# Increment and Hasp edge ahead; none is cleared

**The evidence does not support a single clear winner, so this report names the best two. INCREMENT ranks first and HASP second, with FORENOON and FERRULE as reserves.** INCREMENT has the cleanest register picture and the best fit with a calm, dose-transparent 25 mg brand. It is also the only top-tier name checked against USPTO's own search backend rather than only a third-party mirror. HASP has the strongest "ownable word" and tin-lid story. Its trademark picture is nearly as clean, but it rests on mirror data alone, and its .com is held by a seller we could not price. Six of the ten deep-screened names fall out: RIVET (pending Rivet Global application naming coffee and energy drinks), SCRUPLE (a live 2026 "dose math" pouch pre-order at scruplelabs.com), CALIPER (a live Caliper CBD packet brand claiming the mark), DOSEO (the DOSED family on the same root), ALVANE (a new tryalvane.com "Alvane" Shopify store) and ELEVENSES (existing Class 25 and 43 marks, an operating Tampa cafe and a $9,000 .com). **No name is cleared.** Trademark data is mostly from Trademarkia mirrors, web search was weak so "no brand found" is weak evidence, and no attorney has reviewed anything. Everything below was checked 2026-10-27, and it is findings and open questions, not legal conclusions. Under the project rules, the compliance gate is not yet cleared and no spending on branding is recommended until the owner records that it is. The age policy is not yet recorded, so this report treats it as 21+ ([decisions.md](/home/user/bolt/work/decisions.md)).

INCREMENT's lead is real but not large. Its fit comes from the idea that a user takes one more small step, which matches a low-dose pouch where the customer chooses the count, and it pairs naturally with a printed "25 mg per pouch" line. It sits in neither the nicotine naming pattern (Zyn, Velo, Rogue, Killa) nor the energy vocabulary, and no pouch name in the inventory resembles it ([Roon blog inventory, vendor source, UNVERIFIED](https://www.takeroon.com/blog/who-owns-nicotine-pouch-brands)). Its costs are that it is abstract, it will be a weak image, and the .com belongs to Stripe's wound-down magazine. Whether a dictionary word with a "small increase" meaning is treated as suggestive or descriptive for caffeine goods is a question for counsel, not something these notes settle.

HASP's argument is the opposite. It is a short, firm word with no connection to caffeine, energy or health, and the closing-a-tin-lid image suits a small adult tin. The same hard, short shape sits near nicotine pouch names, a risk the notes attribute to packaging more than to the word. The 25 mg strength is also not unique, since a Grinds 25 mg option is listed in sellers' comparisons ([decisions.md](/home/user/bolt/work/decisions.md)), so a brand name has to carry more of the differentiation.

FORENOON and FERRULE trail for different reasons. FORENOON has the thinnest trademark record of any name screened, one dead mark, but it is a plain dictionary word for morning and may be descriptive of a morning-use product. It also hints at a "wake up" effect that copy would have to avoid. FERRULE has no target-class hits either, but it is a spelling and pronunciation hazard (the one-r "ferule" returned nothing on Trademarkia and is also a different English word) and a crowd of 2026 software and tool users has formed around it.

## The category's own signals shape what a safe name looks like

Existing pouch names cluster in two camps. Nicotine pouches use short, hard-consonant or "cool" words, and caffeine pouches use short coined words or plain terms (Wip, Mojo, Grinds, Roon, NZE, Ultra), per a competitor's vendor blog, so the owner data in it is UNVERIFIED ([Roon blog](https://www.takeroon.com/blog/who-owns-nicotine-pouch-brands)). The notes' own inference is that a differentiating route avoids both the nicotine sound patterns and descriptive energy vocabulary. The four finalists all pass the vocabulary test; HASP, being a one-syllable hard word, is the one that most resembles the nicotine pattern in sound, while INCREMENT and FORENOON do not.

Platform risk is the least-evidenced area. Meta's ad standards bar promoting nicotine pouches and "products that simulate smoking, such as vapes, including products that don't contain tobacco or nicotine", and the retrieved text does not mention caffeine ([Meta](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/tobacco-related-products/)). Google's policy was reachable only through a search summary, and TikTok and payment-processor wording was not retrieved at all. The notes infer, without platform confirmation, that evoking nicotine pouches in name or imagery could raise review risk. None of the four finalists names a tobacco, vape or smoking concept, so the exposure sits in packaging and imagery, which the name cannot fix. One reported school response shows the concern is not hypothetical: a Kansas school treats caffeine pouches under "tobacco and simulated tobacco" rules ([Fortune, 2026-10-22](https://fortune.com/2026/10/22/schools-ban-caffeine-pouches-students-coffee/)).

Youth slang points the same way. The same Fortune article reports nicknames "lip pillows, lip cushies, and upper or lower deckies", and the first pass found a filing named UPPERDECKYS ([Justia](https://trademark.justia.com/986/38/upperdeckys-98638933.html)). The candy, dessert, cartoon and slang names those nicknames imply are the lane to avoid, and none of the four finalists touches it. The cautions are GROMMET (already rejected), JIGGER and GRADUATE (rejected), and any tagline using "deck", "lip" or "cushie". Adult-coded restraint is the posture the evidence favours.

Implied claims separate the finalists. The FTC judges the net impression of all label and ad elements, and FDA treats a brand name that conveys a disease as a claim ([Jones Day on FTC guidance](https://jonesday.com/en/insights/2023/01/ftc-overhauls-and-expands-25yearold-health-products-advertising-compliance-guide), [21 CFR 101.93](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/101.93); both secondary, not the primary texts). Neither source addresses specific words such as energy, focus or calm, so applying them is inference. On that inference, HASP and FERRULE carry the least claim load because neither implies an effect. INCREMENT implies stepwise accumulation, so copy such as "build up your energy" would create a net impression the project's rules forbid. FORENOON implies a morning effect. DOSEO, rightly eliminated, implied dosing as medicine. Any word like energy, focus or calm in a tagline or product line would need review regardless of the name, and that review belongs to the label-claims-reviewer and counsel.

## How much weight the evidence can bear

The evidence is thin at every layer, which is why the report names conditions instead of a winner. The trademark data for HASP, FERRULE, RIVET, DOSEO, ALVANE, FORENOON and ELEVENSES is from Trademarkia, a third-party mirror, and is UNVERIFIED against USPTO. For INCREMENT, SCRUPLE and CALIPER only, the notes also queried the JSON endpoint behind USPTO's own search page (`tmsearch.uspto.gov`). That endpoint is undocumented, may lag recent filings, and is not a clearance search because it has no design-code, translation or similar-meaning logic. Justia returned 403, TSDR and WIPO were unusable, and state registers and Google were not run. The backend did add facts the mirror missed: the Caliper IP Class 32 filing and the full set of INCREMENT hits.

Web search was weak. It missed scruplelabs.com entirely, so "no brand found" for any finalist is weak evidence of absence, and the notes say so repeatedly. Instagram returned 429 or login walls for every name, and Facebook and TikTok were inconsistent, so handles are largely unchecked. TikTok @increment, YouTube and X @hasp and @ferrule, and YouTube @scruple, @caliper and @rivet were observed. Foreign-language meanings are mostly unsourced: the deep screens checked Wiktionary for a few languages and mostly found no entry, and absence of an entry is not proof of no meaning. HASP's Urban Dictionary page had a "Load more" link that was not expanded, as did FERRULE's and RIVET's. For-sale prices were found only for scruple.com ($44,999), getrivet.com ($4,895), tryrivet.com ($2,795), getcaliper.com ($1,988) and elevenses.com ($9,000) on Afternic, with the Elevenses figure resting on one fetch summary. Sedo, Atom and GoDaddy were blocked, so for-sale status of hasp.com, ferrule.com and forenoon.com is undetermined. Fresh filings lag in every database consulted, which matters because several threats (Travex, Coldiron, scruplelabs) are weeks to months old.

## The decision turns on two conditions

**INCREMENT wins if** the attorney's knockout search in the target classes finds nothing that blocks it, including phonetic variants and the INCREMENTAL, INCREMENTUM and INCREMENTS families; if counsel regards the word as acceptable for caffeine pouches; and if the owner accepts that its .com is Stripe's and that the working domains are incrementco.com or incrementpouches.com, with incrementcoffee.com held by an unknown party since January 2026.

**HASP wins if** the same search is clear for HASP; if the Travex HASP app (Class 9 and 45) is judged harmless to a consumer product or Travex proves approachable; if hasp.com can be acquired at a price the owner accepts or the owner is content with haspco.com or hasppouches.com; and if packaging can break away from nicotine-tin conventions. A tie goes to INCREMENT on evidence depth, to HASP on distinctiveness. If both fail counsel's review, FORENOON is the next candidate, then FERRULE.
