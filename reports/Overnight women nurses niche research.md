# Selling to night nurses: viable only if claim-free

**A 25 mg, adults-only (treated as 21+ because `work/decisions.md` records no age) caffeine pouch can reach women nurses who work overnight, but the evidence supports a conditional, test-first "maybe", not a "yes".** The audience is large and plausibly affordable: about 3.47 million RNs, median pay $97,550, and roughly 87-89% women ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/registered-nurses.htm); [USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/answers/how-many-nurses-are-there-in-the-us/)). But the one thing that makes the niche feel obvious, tiredness at 3 a.m., is the thing Bolt's rules forbid the brand to talk about. Every message that connects the product to energy, focus, alertness or "getting through the shift" is out, which leaves price, flavor, format, 21+, ingredients and "25 mg per pouch". On the model, a nurse on three 12-hour shifts a week uses a tin every 9 to 15 days at 3 to 5 pouches per shift, so reorders are slow and payback depends almost entirely on the first order: **a 4-tin first order earns $16.84 contribution at assumed costs, a 1-tin order loses $2.25, and a $40 acquisition cost needs 111 to 185 days to pay back even at 5 pouches per shift.** The owner has chosen this niche, and this report does not reopen that. It does record that the earlier general comparison scored night-shift nurses 21 points against 25 for coffee drinkers and 22 for truckers, on equal weights and with no acquisition-cost or product-cost data ([niche selection report](/home/user/bolt/reports/Caffeine pouch niche selection research.md)). For nurses to work, four things must hold: a claim-free offer still converts at roughly $25 or less acquisition cost per customer; 4-tin bundles dominate first orders; counsel clears occupation-aimed copy and a pregnancy/lactation treatment; and hospital or workplace rules do not turn the pouch into a source of embarrassment for the buyer. None of these is known today. Nothing here recommends spending before the compliance gate is cleared and recorded.

All facts below were checked on 2026-10-27. Items marked UNVERIFIED rest on search summaries, competitor pages or secondary sources; several primary pages (ACOG, CDC, Reddit, Meta's personal-attributes policy) returned errors. Cost and pricing figures marked ASM are assumptions, not data. Nothing here is a legal conclusion.

## The customer is a woman near 50 whose night share nobody has measured

The workforce facts are solid on size and age and thin on the night-shift detail that defines the niche. The NCSBN 2024 study (744,714 RN respondents) found a median age of **50**, 10% men, hospitals as the primary setting for **53%**, median pretax earnings of **$88,000**, and 40% planning to leave the profession within five years ([NCSBN](https://ncsbn.org/research/recent-research/workforce/2024-workforce-rn.page)). The page does not report female share or night share. A secondary summary lists female at 89.3% (UNVERIFIED) ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/workforce/after-years-of-gains-male-rn-representation-fell-to-10-4-5-notes.html)); BLS-derived CPS data gives about 87% ([USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/answers/how-many-nurses-are-there-in-the-us/)). Use "about 87-89%". A different Becker's summary implies about 88% from a 12% male share (UNVERIFIED), which does not match NCSBN's 10%, so the exact figure should not be quoted to a decimal.

Shift patterns are the weakest evidence. BLS says hospital and nursing-care nurses work shifts around the clock including nights ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/registered-nurses.htm)). The only shift-mix data found is a 2009 ANA poll (59.4% on 12-hour shifts, 20.7% on straight 12-hour nights; UNVERIFIED and 17 years old) ([CDC stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/191903/cdc_191903_DS1.pdf)), a learning-provider article that says most full-time nurses work three 12-hour shifts for about 36 hours a week (UNVERIFIED, survey unnamed) ([Elite Learning](https://www.elitelearning.com/?p=280918)), and NCSBN 2022's 70.2% full-time figure via a secondary summary (UNVERIFIED) ([LeadingAge](https://leadingage.org/national-sample-survey-of-registered-nurses/)). The three-shifts-a-week cadence in the model is therefore a labelled assumption.

Caffeine use is near-universal in the studies found, which supports the category fit without supporting any claim. Among 182 nurses, 88.1% of US respondents used caffeine, against 90.8% in Italy and 92% in Korea, and US daily energy-drink use was 11.9% ([Phillips 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9023427/table/Tab1)). In a 476-nurse sample, 22.5% were energy-drink consumers, 62.8% caffeine-only and 14.7% none ([Higbee 2020](https://mattioli1885journals.com/index.php/actabiomedica/article/view/15651)); both are search summaries and UNVERIFIED. Break culture is documented qualitatively: a night-shift study found breaks used for eating, uneven unit support and breaks described as "a luxury, not a right" ([Workplace Health Saf 2021](https://digitalcommons.providence.org/publications/4391)), and a 806-nurse study of 12-hour shifts reported most did not take regular rest breaks (UNVERIFIED) ([OR Manager](https://www.ormanager.com/briefs/rest-break-practices-of-12-hour-shift-nurses/)). A Reddit-derived anecdote of colleagues taking about 500 mg a shift and reporting headaches and anxiety (UNVERIFIED, not an exact quote) suggests peers police excess ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/quality/8-self-care-tips-nurses-wish-they-knew-sooner/)).

What is not known matters as much. There is no primary figure for the share of RNs who work nights, travel or per-diem; no data on how nurses buy caffeine (Amazon, vending, convenience, Costco); no data on pouches per shift, taste dislikes or price tolerance; and **no verbatim nurse language at all**, because Reddit, allnurses, Facebook and TikTok could not be reached. The only "nurse voice" in the notes is competitor copy, which is not nurse speech. Hospital policy on oral pouches at the bedside was not researched; the searches found tobacco-free policies written around smokeless tobacco, vapes and e-cigarettes, none naming oral pouches (UNVERIFIED; summaries only) ([Cameron Memorial](https://www.cameronmch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Tobacco_Free_Campus_Policy.pdf); [Johns Hopkins](https://hopkinsmedicine.org/news/articles/new-tobacco-free-at-work-policy-takes-effect-sept-1)). Schools already treat caffeine pouches as look-alikes of nicotine pouches ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/22/schools-ban-caffeine-pouches-students-coffee/)), so a supervisor or patient challenging a pouch is a plausible customer-experience risk, but that is inference.

## The claim-safe boundary leaves price, format and the number

The messaging that is low-risk, if true and substantiated, is narrow: price, flavor, format and size, "21+", the ingredient list, "25 mg caffeine per pouch", convenience (no spill, pocket-size) and discretion. Discretion needs counsel review because near a workplace or patient area it can read as concealment, and "nicotine-free" or "tobacco-free" invites a nicotine comparison. The risky list is long: energy, focus, alertness, wakefulness, "stay sharp", "stay awake", "get through your shift", "beat fatigue", "no crash", any link to shift schedules, sleep or patient safety, "perform better", "safe", any pregnancy or lactation statement, any statement about being allowed at work, nicotine-pouch look-alike cues, nurse-as-expert endorsement, scrubs or hospital imagery, and product names that imply night or stamina. Addressing the viewer's tiredness ("Exhausted?") also risks Meta's personal-attributes rule, whose current text was not retrievable (the secondary summary is UNVERIFIED) ([Accelerated Digital Media](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)).

**These are inferences for counsel, not agency rules.** No court or agency text was found that classifies any specific phrase above. The reasoning rests on general principles. The FTC says health claims need substantiation "expressly or by implication", that net impression governs and that names and imagery can imply claims ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). If Bolt is a dietary supplement, an effect statement can be a structure/function claim needing the FDA disclaimer and a notice within 30 days of first marketing, and disease-implying statements can make a product a drug ([21 CFR 101.93](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/versioner/v1/full/2026-10-20/title-21.xml?part=101&section=101.93)); whether a non-swallowed pouch is a supplement is unresolved. No FDA or FTC action specifically on "alertness" or "stay awake" wording for caffeine was found: the closest FDA caffeine actions concerned dose risk and the AeroShot inhaler (UNVERIFIED summaries) ([CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fda-warns-aeroshot-caffeine-inhalers-maker-saying-supplements-cant-be-inhaled)).

Competitor nurse copy is the clearest example of what to avoid, and it is also a trap for borrowing. Roon's nurse pages say "Steady focus for 6 to 8 hours", "Clean energy. Calm focus. No crash", "One pouch at 7pm is still working at 2am" and a guarantee that you pay nothing "if you don't feel a real difference in 30 days" ([Roon nurses](https://www.takeroon.com/pouches/nurses-night-shift-focus); [Roon 12-hour shifts](https://www.takeroon.com/pouches/er-nurse-no-drinks-past-the-doors)). Roon's product is 80 mg per pouch, not 25 mg. Ultra, launched September 2026 at 180 mg, says "sharper focus" and "kick in within 5 to 10 minutes" and offers a nurse discount at an unstated percentage ([Uncrate](https://uncrate.com/ultra-energy-pouches/)). Each of these is an effect, speed, duration or "no crash" claim, which breaches Bolt's rule 4. Their existence does not show the claims are permitted, and the notes record no evidence of agency action on them either way. Even Roon's sleep-timing advice ("stop at least 8 hours before you plan to sleep") is a sleep-adjacent statement that counsel would need to approve for any Bolt equivalent.

The fatigue framing adds a reputational layer. ANA's September 1, 2026 statement says fatigue is "not an individual failing", puts responsibility on employers (recovery time, hour limits, predictable scheduling) and lists individual steps such as sleep hygiene and structured breaks; the release does not mention caffeine ([ANA](https://www.nursingworld.org/news/news-releases/2026-news-releases/nurse-fatigue-patient-safety-crisis/)). A brand selling to night nurses as a fatigue fix can read as masking a systems problem that the profession's own association says employers own. NIOSH's nurse training module describes caffeine at shift start as one countermeasure (UNVERIFIED summary) ([NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod7/06.html)); that is government educational content and gives a brand no licence to repeat it. Endorsement adds more: under the FTC Endorsement Guides, free product, discounts and commissions are material connections needing clear disclosure, and an endorser's qualifications must match the expertise they are presented as having (16 CFR 255; the notes flag that section numbering needs counsel confirmation) ([eCFR Part 255](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/versioner/v1/full/2026-10-20/title-16.xml?part=255)). A nurse creator in scrubs praising the pouch risks being read as an implied medical endorsement. State nursing-board limits on promoting products were not found; one state (Maine) incorporates the ANA Code of Ethics into licensee rules (UNVERIFIED) ([Maine Board of Nursing](https://www.maine.gov/boardofnursing/docs/Chapter_14_Final.pdf)).

### Pregnancy, lactation and label items are for counsel; no wording is drafted

Because the target is women, many of childbearing age, the label and website treatment is a planning item for the label-claims-reviewer and counsel. FDA cites **400 mg a day** for most adults and, for those pregnant, trying or breastfeeding, tells them to talk to a health care provider; it gives no numeric limit for that group ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)). The widely reported ACOG figure of under 200 mg a day is **UNVERIFIED** from ACOG itself (page returned 402; the 2010 committee opinion abstract refers to moderate intake under 200 mg) ([PubMed 20664420](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20664420/)). The National Academies 200 mg (pregnancy) and 300 mg (lactation) figures are also UNVERIFIED (search summary) ([National Academies](https://nationalacademies.org/event/01-29-2020/docs/DAD98AEED6500F1587EC30594D568B0901B571C912FE?noSaveAs=1)). The arithmetic is plain: 8 pouches is 200 mg and 12 pouches is 300 mg, and CLAUDE.md already requires a daily-total statement that counsel must approve. Roon carries "not recommended for pregnant or lactating people" wording as a competitor precedent (UNVERIFIED). Prop 65 status for caffeine reproductive toxicity was not confirmed ([OEHHA](https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/chemicals/caffeine)). Also open: FDA labeling guidance for caffeine content is on its 2026 priority list with no date ([Food Dive](https://www.fooddive.com/news/caffeine-labeling-fda-guidance-celsius-alani-nu/824355/)), child-resistance and warning wording, and whether any "safe" or "ask your provider" language is itself a claim.

## The economics hinge on a 3-4 tin first order and a CAC near $25

The model extends the parent unit-economics script and is rerunnable (`nurse_economics.py`, with five CSVs). It computes contribution as order value plus any customer-paid shipping, minus product cost, payment fees (2.9% plus $0.30), 2% refunds, shipping cost, $2.50 pick-pack and any adult-signature fee. **Every cost input is an assumption**, so the tables show the shape, not a forecast. Unsourced inputs: product cost of $1.50, $2.50 or $3.50 per tin; 20 pouches per tin (competitors list 15, 18 or 20); $2.50 pick-pack; 2% refunds and chargebacks; flat shipping of $5.50 or $8.00 regardless of tin count (optimistic); the price ladder; the $4.99 customer shipping option; the $35 and $30 free-shipping thresholds (Roon uses $35 or $45, Nectr $50, all UNVERIFIED); 8% monthly churn (vendor blogs give 5-12%, UNVERIFIED); and any site conversion rate. The 15% subscription discount is also UNVERIFIED (inferred from a competitor). Payment fees come from published Stripe and Shopify pricing.

### Cadence at three shifts a week

Three 12-hour shifts a week is 13.0 shifts a month. The pouches-per-shift figures below are scenarios used to test the model, not goals, and nothing in the brand should suggest a number. FDA's 400 mg is a ceiling, and 8 per shift is 200 mg from pouches alone.

| Pouches per shift | Per month | Tins per month (20 per tin) | One tin lasts | 4-tin bundle lasts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 (75 mg) | 39 | 1.95 | 15.4 days | 61.5 days |
| 5 (125 mg) | 65 | 3.25 | 9.2 days | 36.9 days |
| 8 (200 mg) | 104 | 5.20 | 5.8 days | 23.1 days |

Light, shift-day-only use means reorders come 1.5 to 3 times slower than the parent daily-use model. That favours a skippable "every 4 / 6 / 8 / 10 weeks" subscription with pause and date change over a fixed monthly one, which would over-ship light users and raise cancellations. The 15-pouch tin raises shipping and pick-pack per pouch, so 20 is the better working assumption (no data on nurse preference).

### Bundle and AOV design

At a ladder of $8.99, $8.49, $7.99, $7.99 and $7.49 per tin for 1 to 5 tins (assumed, anchored to sourced multipack prices of $7.50-$8.25 per tin), with product cost $2.50, shipping $5.50 and free shipping on everything, first-order contribution is **-$2.25 for one tin, $2.85 for two, $7.00 for three, $12.09 for four and $14.81 for five**. In the worst case (product cost $3.50, shipping $8.00) three tins earn $1.50 and four earn $5.59. The 3-4 tin floor is therefore firm: a 1-tin order loses money in the base case. Charging customers $4.99 shipping below $35 turns one tin to +$2.49 and two to +$7.59 but risks conversion, which is untested, and a $30 free-shipping line sits just above a 3-tin order and nudges toward four. A 5-tin bundle is a discount trap: at $7.49 per tin it earns $14.81 on the first order, less than the 4-tin's $16.84 when customers pay shipping, and its 76.9-day cycle at 3 per shift delays reorders. A ladder at $10.99 per tin lifts a 4-tin first order to $19.70.

Adult signature, if required or chosen, costs about $9-$11 per parcel (FedEx $10.00 in 2026, UNVERIFIED) ([3PL Center](https://3plcenter.com/shipping-surcharges-an-in-depth-look-at-additional-fees-from-fedex-ups-and-usps/)). At $10 it makes one- and two-tin orders lose money and cuts a 3-tin first order to +$1.74 and a 4-tin to +$6.84. Whether a signature is legally required is not established; California AB 2030, reported signed 2026-09-28, would require online sellers of covered supplements to verify age by government-source data or adult-signature shipping, effective date unknown ([Haynes Boone](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/alerts/new-california-law-bans-sale-of-diet-pills-and-certain-supplements-to-minors)). Shopify age-check apps range from free to about $29 a month, or $0.35 per check (UNVERIFIED listings) ([OneID](https://apps.shopify.com/oneid); [AgeProof](https://apps.shopify.com/ageproof-age-verification)).

A healthcare discount should replace the subscription discount, not stack with it. On a 4-tin free-shipping order it costs $3.04 at 10%, $4.56 at 15% and $6.08 at 20% of first-order contribution. Reorder contribution is $12.28 when the 15% healthcare or subscription discount applies once, against $8.41 when 15% and 15% stack, a 31% drop. Gift bundles (4 tins plus a $2.00 insert, assumed) earn $12.98 at $34.99, $17.73 at $39.99 and $22.49 at $44.99, but gifting needs recipient-age handling, and "thank-you for tired nurses" is the exact framing the rules forbid.

CAC benchmarks are all vendor blogs and none is for nurses or caffeine pouches. Dividing order value by a ROAS of 1.8 to 3.2 gives a first-order CAC of $7.49 to $20.81; dividing a $1.58 or $2.64 click by an assumed 1-3% conversion gives **$53 to $264**. The two methods differ by an order of magnitude, and only a test can say which applies. ### Wholesale to hospital gift shops

A consultant told Clarivate a hospital gift shop must mark up "50% or more" and that about 80% of customers are hospital employees (UNVERIFIED; page returned 403) ([Clarivate](https://clarivate.com/?p=89933)). At an $8.99 retail price and a 50% retailer markup, the brand nets $5.99 and keeps $3.24 per tin at $2.50 cost, with zero CAC. At keystone (100% markup) the brand nets $4.50 and keeps $1.75; adding a 20% distributor (assumed; vendor range 15-35%) drops it to $0.85, and to negative at $3.50 cost. Order sizes, minimums and hospital rules on selling a stimulant pouch on campus are unknown. Wholesale is cheap to probe but its sales cost (samples, time) is not modelled.

## Increment is clinical and Hasp is hard, so packaging does the work

Five claim-free territories are available: price clarity (per-pouch price on the tin, a published ladder), pocket format with discretion, dose transparency ("25 mg, printed on every pouch"), taste, and solidarity through verified-nurse pricing and schedule-aware subscription (skip, pause, change date). Each carries a risk: comparisons need substantiation and a stable comparator; "scrub pocket" and "past the doors" invite on-shift use and the unknown hospital-policy question; "you decide how many" or "stack as needed" reads as a heavy-use invitation; "refreshing" is borderline effect language; "hero" or "fuel for the front line" is fatigue and performance framing. Roon already owns "no cup, no lid, no free hand" for nurses, so differentiation has to come from the 25 mg unit, price per pouch, flavor and tone. Grinds is the only 25 mg comparable found at about $0.33 per pouch, and the 25 mg strength is the weakest part of the price story at roughly $1.32 per 100 mg against $0.28-$0.60 for 100-200 mg rivals (UNVERIFIED) ([niche selection report](/home/user/bolt/reports/Caffeine pouch niche selection research.md)); no nurse-specific 25 mg offer was found.

On name fit, both candidates are uncleared. Increment is the stronger candidate for dose transparency but reads clinical, since nurses meet the word in dosing and titration, and its "one more small step" meaning can nudge toward adding pouches, so copy must avoid "one more", "build up" and "step up". Hasp suits the discretion and lid-clasp territory but sits closest to the short, hard-consonant naming pattern of nicotine pouches and could be misheard as "gasp" or "hash" (inference). The brand report records no live or pending Increment mark in the classes checked on USPTO's backend, a pending HASP filing by Travex LLC in Classes 9 and 45 (mirror data only), and Stripe's ownership of increment.com ([brand name report](/home/user/bolt/reports/Bolt replacement brand name research.md)). These are reads from the notes, not clearances. "Night Shift" in any name, tagline or domain is an implied-claim risk and is off the table pending counsel.

Packaging should avoid the nicotine-tin look: no flat matte-black round tin with a bold single-colour strength band and mint-green or ice-blue coding, no bold uppercase single-word logo with the strength number as the largest element, no lip or mouth imagery, lightning or flame, and no nurse icons or medical symbols. Instead use a plain, large "25 mg caffeine per pouch" panel, an adults-only statement, total milligrams per tin, a warm paper-white or soft earth-tone label with humanist type, and a lid and shape clearly unlike pharmacy blister packs. Warning wording and child-resistance are for counsel.

## Verdict: viable on paper, unproven in practice, with a no-spend path to find out

**The niche can be profitable only under narrow conditions.** The audience is real and the caffeine habit is common, but three things work against it: the strongest message is forbidden, the 25 mg unit is a weak per-milligram value story, and nurses on three shifts a week reorder slowly. It works if (1) a claim-free page converts well enough to hold CAC near or below $25, where 4-tin bundles pay back in 37 to 62 days at 3 to 5 pouches per shift; (2) at least 70% of first orders are 3-4 tins (a threshold from the model, not a source); (3) real product cost stays near $2.50 or below and shipping near $5.50; (4) counsel clears occupation-aimed copy, the endorsement structure and pregnancy/lactation treatment; and (5) hospital gift shops or workplace rules are not a barrier to the buyer. If CAC lands above $60, or 1-2 tin orders dominate, stop or reprice. If the owner will not accept a low-key brand that never says why a nurse might want the product, the niche does not work, because the competitor advantage is in exactly those claims.

1. Desk checks (no cost): counsel reviews the flagged-word list and territories; trademark attorney searches Increment and Hasp; read SheerID and ID.me merchant terms; confirm payment-processor and ad-platform acceptance.
2. Manual reads (no cost): the owner or a researcher opens the Meta Ad Library to see which nurse and caffeine-pouch ads run, reads r/nursing, r/nightshift and r/Nurse rules pages by hand, and checks Meta's current personal-attributes and job-title targeting text.
3. Waitlist smoke test (zero to a few hundred dollars): one claim-free landing page with the price ladder and a notify-me button, no payment taken for product that does not exist; compare the five territory headlines by clicks and signups, and read bundle preference from clicks.
4. Adult-nurse taste panel (small): free samples to a consenting group of verified 21+ nurses with a taste, comfort and "does this look like a nicotine tin" survey; counsel clears sampling first.
5. Pre-sell (after cost quotes): 4-tin bundles at $7.99 versus $9.99 per tin, split, to measure conversion and cancellations.
6. Wholesale conversations: three to five hospital gift shops or cafes with a two-line sell sheet, asking order size, margin and policy.

Break-even thresholds from the model: first-order CAC at or below **$16.84** is a clear green light at 4 tins; **$25 to $40** needs 4+ tin mix and a reorder inside the cycle; above **$60** stop or reprice. At $60, only about 8 pouches per shift pays back within 92 days (4-tin bundle), and brand rules do not allow engineering for that.

### For a professional

Lawyer (food and drug, advertising): whether occupation-targeted claims or imagery, or "night shift" wording, create an implied alertness or fatigue claim; whether a nurse endorser, a nurse title or scrubs imagery is an implied medical endorsement under 16 CFR 255 and state board rules; whether hospital or workplace policy exposes the brand or the buyer; whether adult-signature delivery is required before the $9-$11 fee; whether the age gate (checkbox, ID check, government-source verification) is adequate given AB 2030; how to word the daily total, pregnancy and lactation statements and any guarantee; sampling rules; ad-platform and processor acceptance; whether verified-nurse pricing and gifting to a possibly under-age recipient raise issues. The label-claims-reviewer step: every headline direction, the flagged-word list and the packaging brief, saved in `work/label-claims-reviewer/`. Accountant: confirmation of cost, 3PL and processing assumptions, and verification-platform contract cost. Insurance broker: product liability for a stimulant sold to a safety-sensitive occupation. Trademark attorney: Increment and Hasp clearance and the "Nurses Week" and association-mark use.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question: the niche is not blocked by the audience but by the gap between what drives nurses to buy caffeine and what Bolt may say. What survives is a price, format and dose-transparency brand with a verified-nurse price and a slow, skippable subscription, where acquisition cost and bundle mix, not the dose per pouch, decide profit. Light shift-day use makes the bundle floor of three to four tins and a CAC near $25 a stricter test than the parent model suggested.

The most useful next step is cheap and mostly manual: read the platform and subreddit rules, put a claim-free page in front of a small adult-nurse panel, and measure bundle mix before any inventory. The biggest unknowns are still the least researched ones: the night-shift share of RNs, real nurse language, hospital policy on oral pouches, and the true CAC. Until those close, the verdict stays conditional.
