# Workplace, regulatory and claims constraints on selling a 25 mg caffeine pouch (21+) to women night-shift nurses (US)

Date checked for every item: 2026-10-27. Findings for the owner and counsel, not legal advice. Nothing here concludes that anything is legal, compliant or safe. Tools: WebSearch (returns summaries) and WebFetch; Firecrawl unavailable. Items from search summaries only, or from secondary sources, are marked UNVERIFIED. About 20 tool calls were used; several questions are only partly answered (see Gaps).

## 1. Hospital and employer policies (tobacco-free rules, look-alike risk, bedside rules)

### Takeaway
No hospital, health-system or nursing-school policy that mentions caffeine pouches or oral pouches was found. Existing tobacco-free policies are written around tobacco, smokeless tobacco and vapes, so whether a nicotine-free pouch is caught depends on each policy's wording and on how an enforcer perceives it.

### Cited Findings
- A search for hospital tobacco-free policies found none that name nicotine pouches or oral pouches; the closest policies cover smokeless tobacco, chewing tobacco, snuff, e-cigarettes and ENDS. UNVERIFIED (search summary only; the policy PDFs were not opened) — [search results incl. Cameron Memorial](https://www.cameronmch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Tobacco_Free_Campus_Policy.pdf), [Independence Health](https://independence.health/patients-visitors/visitor-information/tobacco-free-campus), [Michigan DHHS policy](https://dhhs.michigan.gov/OLMWEB/EX/AP/Public/APF/112.pdf), checked 2026-10-27
- Johns Hopkins' tobacco-free-at-work policy, per the search summary, bars employees from cigarettes, cigars, pipes, chewing tobacco, e-cigarettes and vape pens, including on meals or breaks, on or off campus. UNVERIFIED (summary only) — [Johns Hopkins Medicine](https://hopkinsmedicine.org/news/articles/new-tobacco-free-at-work-policy-takes-effect-sept-1), checked 2026-10-27
- A search for "caffeine pouches nurses hospital policy" returned only school-ban and youth-health stories, no hospital or nursing-school policy — [CP24/AP, 2026-10-22](https://www.cp24.com/news/world/2026/10/22/some-us-schools-are-banning-caffeine-pouches-heres-what-parents-and-students-should-know/), checked 2026-10-27
- Look-alike perception is documented in schools: one Michigan middle school said pouches "closely resemble nicotine pouches" and a Kansas district treats them under tobacco rules (as reported in the existing launch report; not re-verified here) — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/22/schools-ban-caffeine-pouches-students-coffee/), [ABC7 Chicago](https://abc7chicago.com/post/what-are-caffeine-pouches-schools-banning/19169168/), cited in /home/user/bolt/reports/Bolt caffeine pouch launch research.md
- A 2021 Applied Nursing Research study found many nurses consume caffeine at work and that effects on clinical performance and patient safety are unclear; it covered energy drinks and caffeine generally, not pouches. UNVERIFIED (summary) — [Ewha repository entry](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/caffeine-and-high-energy-drink-use-and-knowledge-by-nurses-in-thr/), checked 2026-10-27

### Inferences
- A policy that bans "tobacco-like", "smokeless" or "oral" products, or that tells staff not to hold anything in the mouth on the unit, could catch a pouch in practice even with no nicotine. This is inference; no source tested it.
- Because of the look-alike issue, a customer using a Bolt pouch near a patient-care area could be challenged by a supervisor or patient. That is a customer-experience and reputation risk for the brand, not a legal conclusion.

### Gaps
- Joint Commission and CMS requirements: no source retrieved. Tobacco-free campus is generally a hospital policy choice, but no primary text was found; counsel should check.
- Infection-control or uniform rules about items in the mouth at the bedside: nothing found.
- Nursing-school (student) policies mentioning oral pouches: none found.
- Actual policy text naming "oral pouches" with URL: none found; absence in a general search is not proof none exists.

## 2. Fatigue and patient-safety framing; why not to sell it as a fatigue fix

### Takeaway
Fatigue in nurses is treated by ANA as a systems and employer problem, and the safety framing makes "alertness" claims especially sensitive. Under FDA rules an alertness or energy statement can be a structure/function claim needing the disclaimer and a notice, and FTC requires substantiation. No FDA or FTC action specifically on "alertness" or "stay awake" claims for caffeine products was found.

### Cited Findings
- ANA's September 1, 2026 statement "Addressing Nurse Fatigue to Promote Health, Safety, and Well-Being for All" (first major update since 2014) frames fatigue as "not an individual failing", puts responsibility on employers (recovery time between shifts, limits on hours, predictable scheduling, nonpunitive reporting) and lists individual steps (sleep hygiene, structured breaks, monitoring personal fatigue). The release does not mention caffeine — [ANA news release](https://www.nursingworld.org/news/news-releases/2026-news-releases/nurse-fatigue-patient-safety-crisis/), checked 2026-10-27
- ANA's 2014 statement, per a secondary summary, listed short naps, caffeine, or both as possible countermeasures and recommended shifts of 12 hours or less. UNVERIFIED (the ANA URL for the original returned 404; summary is from a patient-safety blog) — [Patient Safety Solutions copy](https://www.patientsafetysolutions.com/docs/December_2_2014_ANA_Position_Statement_on_Nurse_Fatigue.pdf), checked 2026-10-27
- A news report on the 2026 statement says 75% to 80% of nurses reported moderate to high fatigue in 2021. UNVERIFIED (secondary; not seen on the ANA release) — [Fierce Healthcare / American Nurse summaries](https://www.myamericannurse.com/nurse-fatigue-the-silent-epidemic/), checked 2026-10-27
- NIOSH's nurse work-hour training module (per search summary) describes prophylactic naps, short in-shift naps, and caffeine at the start of a shift, and says napping plus caffeine improved self-rated alertness in studies. NIOSH also warns unplanned dozing can cause errors. UNVERIFIED (summary; page not fetched) — [NIOSH module 7](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod7/06.html), checked 2026-10-27. Note this is government educational content about caffeine as a countermeasure; it does not license a brand to make the same statement for its own product.
- Structure/function rules: a dietary supplement label statement needs the text "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."; the marketer must notify FDA no later than 30 days after first marketing; statements implying an effect on a disease are disease claims and can make the product a drug — [21 CFR 101.93, eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/versioner/v1/full/2026-10-20/title-21.xml?part=101&section=101.93), checked 2026-10-27. This applies only if Bolt is a dietary supplement, which remains unresolved (see the launch report).
- FTC: health claims need substantiation "expressly or by implication"; net impression governs; product names and imagery can imply claims — [FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance), checked 2026-10-27 (per owner brief and niche_occupational.md; no alertness-specific section found)
- FDA enforcement involving caffeine found by search: AeroShot (2012, FDA objected to "energy" marketing of an inhalable caffeine product on the ground it was a swallowed-only supplement); powdered and liquid caffeine letters (2015, 2018, 2021) focused on dose risk, not alertness wording. None quotes "alertness" or "stay awake". UNVERIFIED (summaries and news) — [CBS on AeroShot](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fda-warns-aeroshot-caffeine-inhalers-maker-saying-supplements-cant-be-inhaled), [FDA Liquidcaffeine.com letter 2018](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/liquidcaffeinecom-545456-05292018), [FDA Trippo letter 2021](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/trippo-international-llc-612586-03102021), checked 2026-10-27
- State overtime/fatigue laws: lists of states restricting mandatory nurse overtime vary (17 or 18 states) and rely on 2007-2012 articles; a Vermont Senate matrix dated 2026-02-12 compares state laws. UNVERIFIED lists — [Vermont S.277 matrix](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/Senate%20Economic%20Development/Bills/S.277/Drafts,%20Amendments,%20and%20Legal%20Documents/S.277~Sophie%20Zdatny~State%20by%20State%20Matrix%20~2-12-2026.pdf), [Littler on Rhode Island](https://littler.com/node/25372), checked 2026-10-27

### Inferences
- Selling to night-shift nurses by reference to their tiredness invites an implied alertness/fatigue-benefit claim (FTC net-impression test), possibly a structure/function claim (101.93), and a patient-safety optic: a product marketed as helping nurses cope with fatigue can read as masking an employer-system problem that ANA says is not an individual failing.
- A product name, tagline or imagery implying night-shift stamina is an implied claim risk per the FTC guide's treatment of names and imagery.

### Gaps
- Joint Commission fatigue guidance (Sentinel Event Alert 48): not retrieved.
- NIOSH page not fetched in full; no dose or timing detail.
- No FDA/FTC action on "alertness" or "stay awake" wording found; FDA warning-letter database not queried directly.
- Current verified list of state mandatory-overtime laws: not obtained.

## 3. Endorsements by nurses and use of the nurse identity

### Takeaway
FTC rules require disclosure of material connections and that an expert endorser's qualifications match what is claimed; the advertiser is liable for endorsers' misleading statements. Nurse-title use in ads is a state board and ethics question that could not be answered for the US.

### Cited Findings
- 16 CFR 255.3(a): "the endorser's qualifications must in fact give the endorser the expertise that the endorser is represented as possessing" — [eCFR Part 255](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/versioner/v1/full/2026-10-20/title-16.xml?part=255), checked 2026-10-27. (The fetch summary attributed this to 255.3; counsel should confirm the section numbering.)
- 255.5: undisclosed connections between endorser and seller must be disclosed when they could affect weight or credibility, including when a significant minority of the audience does not understand or expect the connection — same source.
- 255.1(d): advertisers are liable for misleading or unsubstantiated statements made through endorsements — same source.
- 255.4 covers organizational endorsements (the endorsement must reflect the organization's collective judgment) — same source; relevant if any nursing association were to be named.
- Maine's nursing board incorporates the ANA Code of Ethics for Nurses (2025 edition) into licensee rules, so ethics standards can be enforceable through a license in at least that state. UNVERIFIED (search summary) — [Maine Board of Nursing Chapter 14](https://www.maine.gov/boardofnursing/docs/Chapter_14_Final.pdf), checked 2026-10-27
- By analogy only: the AANA (nurse anesthetists) code limits endorsements to products the nurse is personally satisfied with and bars exploiting the title for unrelated products; Ontario's nursing college says ads must be truthful and verifiable. Neither is a US general-nurse rule. UNVERIFIED (search summary) — [Ethiopia MoH course page quoting AANA code](https://training-covid.moh.gov.et/media/courses/level5-module11/06_30732_en.html), [CNO advertising guidance](https://cno.org/standards-learning/ask-practice/advertising), checked 2026-10-27

### Inferences
- Paying or gifting product to working nurses for posts triggers 255.5 disclosure; presenting a nurse as an expert on staying alert would be an expert-endorsement and claim problem at once.
- Using scrubs, hospital settings or the title "nurse" in ads could imply clinical endorsement; this is a net-impression question for counsel.

### Gaps
- ANA Code of Ethics text on endorsements: not retrieved. US state board rules on the RN title in product ads: not found.
- Trademark rules on nursing association marks (ANA logo, "Nurses Week"): not researched.
- HIPAA/workplace photography: not researched (owner noted not relevant to marketing); filming in clinical settings was not checked.

## 4. Women-specific, pregnancy and lactation, Prop 65

### Takeaway
US-facing sources give about 200 mg/day (ACOG, pregnancy) and about 300 mg/day (lactation, Dietary Guidelines), versus 400 mg/day for healthy adults (FDA). A target group of women of childbearing age makes label and pregnancy/lactation wording a counsel item. No FTC or FDA position on marketing caffeine to women of childbearing age was found.

### Cited Findings
- ACOG Committee Opinion 462 (2010): moderate caffeine intake (under 200 mg/day) does not appear to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth; growth restriction relationship undetermined — [PubMed 20664420](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20664420/), checked 2026-10-27. The current ACOG web page was not retrieved.
- Secondary summaries: recommendations for pregnant and lactating women have been under 200 mg and 300 mg/day respectively; the 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines say caffeine passes into breast milk in small amounts and usually does not adversely affect infants at about 300 mg or less. UNVERIFIED (secondary; 2025-2030 Guidelines wording not retrieved) — [NCBI Bookshelf NBK562624 and related results](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK562624/), [Daily Coffee News on the 2020 Guidelines](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2020/12/31/here-is-every-mention-of-coffee-in-the-new-dietary-guidelines-for-americans), checked 2026-10-27
- FDA's 400 mg/day figure for healthy adults — [FDA consumer update](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much), per the launch report, checked 2026-10-27
- Prop 65: OEHHA's caffeine page lists reproductive toxicity as "under consideration" with a 2008 date and not as listed; per search summary. UNVERIFIED (page not loaded; current status unconfirmed) — [OEHHA caffeine](https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/chemicals/caffeine), checked 2026-10-27

### Inferences
- If a woman uses 8 pouches (200 mg) from pouches alone, she reaches the 200 mg pregnancy figure before any coffee. The 400 mg dose table in the launch report implies this arithmetic; whether and how to print pregnancy/lactation advice is for counsel.
- Targeting women specifically makes any "safe in pregnancy" implication unavailable; the safe-side practice is to avoid any pregnancy statement beyond whatever counsel approves.

### Gaps
- FDA's own pregnancy/lactation wording; current ACOG page; 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines text; CDC/NIH statements: not retrieved.
- FTC/FDA position on marketing caffeine to women of childbearing age: none found.
- Prop 65 ingredient screening for the actual formula: not possible without the formula.

## 5. Platform angles: targeting nurses by job title

### Takeaway
Meta's health and wellness policy requires dietary/health product ads to target ages 18+. Whether targeting by "nurse" job title is allowed could not be confirmed. TikTok and Google rules for caffeine pouches were not verified.

### Cited Findings
- Meta: ads for dietary, health or weight-loss products must be targeted to people at least 18 years old; general food including protein products is exempt; ads cannot make "statements of inferiority about physical appearance"; clickbait promising outcomes within a set time is barred — [Meta health and wellness policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness), checked 2026-10-27 (fetched)
- Meta removed sensitive-topic detailed targeting (health causes, etc.) effective 2022-01-19; reports do not say whether job-title targeting remains. UNVERIFIED — [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/?p=378095), [Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/facebook-removed-thousands-of-detailed-targeting-options/amp/), checked 2026-10-27
- Meta's tobacco policy bars products that "simulate smoking" even without tobacco or nicotine (per launch report) — [Meta tobacco policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/tobacco-related-products/), checked 2026-10-27 (as cited in the launch report)
- Meta personal-attributes policy: the URL tried returned 404; a secondary guide says ads may not assert or imply knowledge of a user's personal attributes, and that second-person phrasing is discouraged. UNVERIFIED — [Accelerated Digital Media guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/), checked 2026-10-27
- TikTok: its ad policy handbook points to industry entry guidelines; TikTok bans weight-loss supplement ads (2020); nothing found on caffeine or energy products. UNVERIFIED — [TikTok Ad Policy Handbook, North America](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/ad-policy-handbook-north-america?lang=en), checked 2026-10-27

### Inferences
- Copy such as "Night-shift nurse tired?" or "Are you exhausted?" could breach the personal-attributes rule (implying knowledge of a person's condition) as well as the alertness-claim rule. Inference from the secondary summary; confirm with Meta's policy text.

### Gaps
- Meta's current position on job-title targeting; Meta's rules for caffeine pouches specifically; Google and TikTok caffeine-pouch rules; no nurse-specific platform rule found.

## 6. Age gating at the point of sale and workplace

### Takeaway
There is no federal minimum age (AP, 2026-10-22) and many Shopify age-gate apps exist at low cost, but most are self-declaration or manual review. Which level counsel would want, given California AB 2030 style online-seller rules, is unresolved.

### Cited Findings
- No federal minimum purchase age for caffeine pouches; some school districts are banning them — [CP24/AP, 2026-10-22](https://www.cp24.com/news/world/2026/10/22/some-us-schools-are-banning-caffeine-pouches-heres-what-parents-and-students-should-know/), checked 2026-10-27
- Shopify app listings (prices as listed, may have changed): OneID $0.35 per check ($0.45 persist) with a free plan; HonestGate $30/year with manual ID review; AgeProof $29/month or $290/year with a checkout block; GateKeeper free, $9.99 or $29.99/month; ProofAge $15/month; AgeCheck $39 one-time; ApexAge $2/month. UNVERIFIED (listing summaries) — [OneID](https://apps.shopify.com/oneid), [AgeProof](https://apps.shopify.com/ageproof-age-verification), [HonestGate](https://apps.shopify.com/age-gate-app-1), [GateKeeper](https://apps.shopify.com/gatekeeper-4), [ProofAge](https://apps.shopify.com/proofage), [AgeCheck](https://apps.shopify.com/agecheck), checked 2026-10-27
- California AB 2030 (reported signed 2026-09-28) would require online sellers of covered supplements to verify age by government-source data or adult-signature shipping; effective date unknown (from launch report) — [Haynes Boone](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/alerts/new-california-law-bans-sale-of-diet-pills-and-certain-supplements-to-minors), as cited in launch report

### Inferences
- A pop-up date-of-birth click is the cheapest tier but is self-declaration; checkout-blocking ID checks cost more per order. Which is adequate is for counsel.

### Gaps

## 7. Claim-safe messaging boundary list (for counsel to review, not legal advice)

### Takeaway
The sources support a split between factual product statements and anything touching physiological effect, tiredness, shifts or safety.

### Cited Findings (what the sources lean on)
- FTC: net impression, names, imagery and implied claims are judged along with express claims; substantiation needed for health benefit claims — [FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)
- FDA: structure/function statements require disclaimer and notice; disease-implying claims can make a drug — [21 CFR 101.93](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/versioner/v1/full/2026-10-20/title-21.xml?part=101&section=101.93)
- Meta: results-timeframe and clickbait language is barred — [Meta health and wellness](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness)
- ANA: fatigue is a systems issue, "not an individual failing" — [ANA 2026](https://www.nursingworld.org/news/news-releases/2026-news-releases/nurse-fatigue-patient-safety-crisis/)

### Inferences (proposed lists, derived from the above; all dates 2026-10-27)
- Generally lower risk, if true and substantiated: price; flavor; format and size; "21+"; ingredient list; "25 mg caffeine per pouch"; "tobacco-free" and "nicotine-free" as factual ingredient statements (counsel to decide, since it invites nicotine comparison); convenience (no spill, pocket-size); discretion (counsel to review, since "discreet" near a workplace or patient area could imply concealment).
- Higher risk: energy, focus, alertness, wakefulness, "stay sharp", "stay awake", "get through your shift", "beat fatigue", "no crash" (project rule bars it), any link to shift schedules, sleep, patient safety, "perform better", "reliable energy for nurses", "safe", any pregnancy or lactation statement, any statement about being allowed at work, any look-alike nicotine-pouch cues, nurse-as-authority endorsement, scrubs or hospital imagery implying clinical endorsement, product names implying night/stamina.
- Also avoid: addressing the viewer's tiredness ("Exhausted?"), which risks both a claim and Meta's personal-attributes rule.

### Gaps
- No court or agency text was found that classifies any specific phrase above; the lists are inference from general principles.
